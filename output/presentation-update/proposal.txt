CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Background 
Neonatal mortality remains a significant public health challenge in Uganda and across sub-Saharan 
Africa. Two preventable and closely monitorable conditions, hypothermia and hypoxia, contribute 
substantially to this burden, particularly in rural clinics and home-birth settings where continuous 
vital-sign monitoring is limited by cost, staffing, and infrastructure. 
A community-based cross-sectional study conducted in Lira district, Northern Uganda, found that 
51.0% of neonates (678 of 1,330) exper ienced hypothermia within their first three days of life, 
with home birth, low birth weight, and delayed breastfeeding initiation identified as statistically 
significant risk factors (Mukunya et al., 2021).  
Detection in that study relied on intermittent h ome visit spot checks using a digital thermometer, 
even under research conditions, continuous monitoring was absent. This mirrors the reality in most 
rural Ugandan clinics, where multi -parameter monitors, when available at all, are commercial 
devices that assume stable mains power and network connectivity neither of which can be 
guaranteed in these settings. 
Existing efforts to address this gap, reviewed in detail in Chapter 2, have tended to address only 
one dimension of the problem at a time. Single -parameter devices such as wearable temperature 
alarms, cloud -dependent multi -parameter systems that assume reliable connectivity, or AI 
techniques applied narrowly to incubator climate control rather than portable ward -level early 
warning. This project proposes  a system that combines multi -parameter sensing, offline -first 
operation, and explainable AI -assisted early warning in a single, low -cost, locally appropriate 
device. 
1.2 Problem Statement 
Rural Ugandan clinics lack affordable, continuous, multi -parameter neonatal monitoring capable 
of functioning reliably without dependable power or network infrastructure. Consequently, 
deterioration from hypothermia or hypoxia frequently goes undetected between manual 
observation rounds, delaying clinical response. Existi ng commercial and academic monitoring 
solutions either address only a single vital sign, depend on continuous cloud connectivity to 
function, or apply artificial intelligence narrowly to equipment climate control rather than to early 
clinical warning.  
This leaves a specific, unaddressed gap for a portable, offline -capable, multi-parameter device 
with explainable AI-assisted early warning suited to the rural Ugandan context.

1.3 Main Objective 
To design, develop, test and evaluate a low -cost, offline-capable embedded neonatal monitoring 
device that provides real-time, on-device early-warning alerts for hypothermia and hypoxia, with 
backend-assisted AI risk scoring and trend analysis when network access is available. 
1.3.1 Specific Objectives 
Design (Hardware) 
● To design the device hardware by interfacing the MAX30102 (SpO2 and heart rate) and 
GY-906 MLX90614 (non -contact temperature) sensors, a buzzer, an LED, a 16x2 I2C 
LCD and a DS3231 real -time clock with an ESP32 microcontroller for sensing, local 
alerting, display and offline timekeeping. 
Develop (Software) 
● To develop the firmware, Flask and PostgreSQL backend with explainable AI risk scoring, 
and React dashboard that together provide on-device threshold alerts, offline buffering with 
automatic synchronisation, and risk-prioritised live and historical monitoring. 
Test 
● To test the hardware components and the software (device sketch, backend and dashboard), 
individually and as an integrated system, through connectivity, end -to-end dataflow, API 
and security tests. 
Evaluate 
● To evaluate the data obtained from the tests and present the metrics involved: real -time 
performance, sampling accuracy, on -device alert latency and jitter, and, for the AI layer 
against a threshold-only baseline, false-alarm rate and detection lead time. 
1.4 Significance 
Given that over half of neonates in a comparable Ugandan district were found to experience 
hypothermia within days of birth (Mukunya et al., 2021), a device demonstrating even a partial 
improvement in early detection ca rries meaningful clinical significance. For clinical officers and 
nurses, an AI-prioritised, offline-capable monitor reduces the reliance on infrequent manual checks 
and directs limited staff attention to the patients who need it most.  
For the health syst em, the proposed device offers a substantially lower -cost alternative to 
commercial multi -parameter monitors, using components realistically available in Uganda. 
Academically, the project contributes a documented, evidence -grounded design and evaluation 
methodology, particularly the comparison between threshold -only and AI -assisted alerting, that 
extends beyond this course and could inform future student or research projects in the same space. 
1.5 Scope and Limitations 
5 
 
This project produces a screening and early -warning aid, not a diagnostic or certified medical 
device, and does not replace clinical judgement. It detects physiological signs correlated with 
hypothermia and hypoxia; it does not diagnose their underlying ca uses. The working prototype 
targets a single active device -patient session at a time, though the underlying data model is 
designed to support multiple concurrent devices and patients. The prototype will not be tested on 
real neonates.  
Testing will use student volunteers or clearly labelled simulated data. The AI models are trained 
on synthetically generated, threshold and scenario derived labelled data rather than real clinical 
outcome data, since no such dataset is available to the project team. 
 
CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW 
2.1 Literature Review 
2.1.1 Epidemiological Context: Where the Problem Comes From 
The clearest evidence that this problem is real and severe in the Ugandan context comes from 
Mukunya et al. (2021), a community-based cross-sectional study of 1,330 neonates in Lira district, 
Northern Uganda. The study found a 51.0% incidence of neonatal hypothermia (defined per WHO 
thresholds: mild 36.0-36.49°C, moderate 32.0-35.99°C, severe below 32.0°C) within the first three 
days of life, with home bir th (adjusted risk ratio 1.9), low birth weight (ARR 1.7), and delayed 
breastfeeding initiation (ARR 1.2) identified as significant risk factors. Detection relied on 
periodic home-visit spot-checks with a digital thermometer, even under research conditions with 
trained field workers, monitoring was intermittent, not continuous. This single study substantiates 
both the scale of the problem and the specific inadequacy of intermittent manual checking that this 
project's continuous, offline-first design directly addresses. 
2.1.2 Related Work: Existing Monitoring and AI-Assisted Solutions 
Several existing efforts address parts of this problem. McAbee et al. (2023) conducted a clinical 
field evaluation of the BEMPU TempWatch, a wearable temperature -alarm bracelet, in Ghana. 
Across 1,973 temperature readings from 249 neonates, the device achieved 67.8% sensitivity and 
95.9% specificity for detecting hypothermia, with sensitivity notably lower among neonates in 
incubators (58.4%) and those with very low birth weight (62.8%).  
This is a valuable real -world benchmark. Even a purpose -built, field-validated single-parameter 
device misses roughly one in three hypothermic episodes, which motivates a multi -parameter 
approach that does not rely on a single threshold crossing to detect deterioration. 
De et al. (2016) proposed a neonatal health monitoring system using Sensor Mobile Cloud 
Computing (SMCC), combining temperature, acceleration, and heart -rate sensors with wireless 
transmission to a cloud backend and Android-based alerts.  
6 
 
While multi-parameter and wireless, the design assumes continuous cloud connectivity for both 
processing and storage, an assumption that does not hold in the rural, connectivity -constrained 
clinics this project targets. Similarly, Abd El-Aziz and Taloba (2021) developed an IoT-based real 
time monitoring system for neonatal incubators using an Arduino Uno, tracking pulse rate, 
temperature, humidity, and gas levels, transmitted wirelessly to clinical staff. This system is 
incubator-specific and, like t he SMCC design, depends on continuous connectivity rather than 
offline-first operation. 
On the AI side, Rahmawati et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review of nine studies applying 
artificial intelligence to neonatal hypothermia management. The review fo und that most 
applications used fuzzy logic controllers for incubator climate regulation (temperature and 
humidity control) rather than for patient-facing early-warning alerting, with one notable exception 
applying artificial neural networks to sepsis diag nosis using laboratory parameters (CRP, 
leukocyte count) not available in a low -resource point -of-care embedded device. No reviewed 
study combined multi-parameter ward-level monitoring, offline operation, and explainable trend-
based early warning.

2.1.4 Research Gap 
No reviewed solution combines offline-first operation, multi-parameter sensing (heart rate, SpO2, 
and temperature), evidence-based localised thresholds, and explainable AI-assisted early warning 
specifically designed for the rural Ugandan context. The BEMPU TempWatch is offline -capable

control or require laboratory data unavailable at the point of care. This project's contribution is to 
close that specific, evidenced gap.

CHAPTER 3: METHODOLOGY 
3.1 Overview of the Approach 
This chapter descr ibes how each specific objective stated in Section 1.3.1 will be achieved. The 
objectives are grouped into four stages, Design, Develop, Test and Evaluate, and the methodology 
follows the same order. The project follows an iterative, milestone -based prototyping approach, 
so that each hardware and software layer is validated before the next is built on top of it, rather 
than integrating untested components at the end. Requirements are drawn from the literature 
reviewed in Chapter 2, including the neonatal hy pothermia thresholds and vital -sign reference 
ranges cited there, and from the practical constraints of clinical officers and nurses working in 
rural, low-resource clinics. 
The technology stack is: Arduino C/C++ on the ESP32 for firmware (Arduino IDE or VS  Code); 
HTTP over WiFi with JSON payloads for device-to-backend communication; Flask (Python) with 
Flask-SQLAlchemy and PostgreSQL for the backend; scikit -learn, pandas and joblib for the AI 
layer; React with Chart.js for the dashboard; and Git/GitHub for version control. 
3.2 Design (Hardware) 
The design stage produces the device hardware. Components are selected against three criteria: 
affordability and availability in Uganda, safety for use near a neonate (3.3V sensor operation and 
non-contact infrared temperature sensing), and suitability for offline-first operation. 
Specific objective: To design the device hardware by interfacing the MAX30102 (SpO2 and heart 
rate) and GY-906 MLX90614 (non-contact temperature) sensors, a buzzer, an LED, a 16x2 I2C 
LCD and a DS3231 real -time clock with an ESP32 microcontroller for sensing, local alerting, 
display and offline timekeeping. The two parts below cover the sensing side and the alert, display 
and timekeeping side of this single objective. 
3.2.1 Interfacing the Sensors with the ESP32 
Objective: To design the sensing hardware by interfacing the MAX30102 and GY -906 
MLX90614 sensors with an ESP32 microcontroller. 
How it will be achieved: Both sensors communicate over I2C, so they will be connected to the 
ESP32 I2C pins (SDA and SCL) on a shared bus with their distinct addresses, powered from the 
3.3V rail with pull-up resistors where required. A wiring schematic will be drawn before assembly, 
and the circuit will first be built on a breadboard so that each sensor can be brought up on its own, 
confirming that the ESP32 detects its I2C address and returns plausible values, before both are run 
together on the same bus. The MAX30102 will be read for heart rate and SpO2 using its red and 
infrared channels, and the MLX90614 for non-contact skin temperature. Sensor placement (finger 
or foot for the pulse oximeter, a fixed short distance for the infrared thermometer) will be fixed in 
a simple mount so that readings are repeatable.

3.2.2 Alert, Display and Timekeeping Hardware 
Part of the objective: To design the local alert, display and timekeeping hardware, comprising 
the buzzer, LED, 16x2 I2C LCD and DS3231 real-time clock. 
How it will be achieved: The 16x2 LCD and the battery-backed DS3231 will share the same I2C 
bus as the sensors, with each device on a unique address. The active buzzer and the LED will be 
driven from ESP32 GPIO pins, with a current-limiting resistor for the LED and a transistor driver 
if the buzzer draws more current than a GPIO pin can supply. The DS3231 will be set once to the 
correct time and will keep it on its coin cell independently of WiFi, so readings can be timestamped 
correctly even after long offline periods. The complete circuit will be documented in a schematic 
and wiring table, powered from a USB 5V source, and then moved from the breadboard to a neat, 
stable prototype layout.

How the prototype is connected, part by part.  
ESP32 and power. The ESP32 development board sits at the top left of the breadboard. Its USB 
cable supplies 5V, which powers the board and is also used to upload the firmware and read serial 
output during testing. The red and black jumper wires carry power and ground from the ESP32 to 
the breadboard power rails, so that every module shares one common ground. The 3.3V pin feeds 
the sensors and the clock, and the 5V pin feeds the LCD backpack. 
Shared I2C bus. The green and yellow jumper wires run from the ESP32 SDA (GPIO 21) and 
SCL (GPIO 22) pins to a common  row on the breadboard. The MAX30102, the MLX90614, the 
DS3231 and the LCD backpack are all attached to these two lines, and the ESP32 tells them apart 
by their I2C addresses (Table 3.1). This is why only two GPIO pins are needed for four devices. 
MAX30102 pulse oximeter (red board, bottom left). Its VCC and GND pins connect to the 3.3V 
and ground rails and its SDA and SCL pins to the shared bus. The finger or foot is placed over the 
sensor window. The red and infrared light reflected through the skin is me asured by the 
photodetector, and the ESP32 reads these raw values over I2C and calculates heart rate and SpO2 
from them. 
GY-906 MLX90614 infrared temperature sensor (yellow board, bottom centre). It is 
connected to the same 3.3V, ground, SDA and SCL lines.  Pointed at the skin from a short fixed 
distance, it measures the infrared radiation from the skin without touching it, and the ESP32 reads 
the temperature over I2C. 

13 
 
DS3231 real-time clock (blue board with coin cell, bottom centre). It is connected to the same 
3.3V, ground, SDA and SCL lines. Its CR2032 cell keeps the time when the device is unpowered 
or offline. The ESP32 reads the time from it each cycle and attaches it to the heart rate, SpO2 and 
temperature values, so that every reading has a correct timestamp even during long offline periods. 
16x2 LCD with I2C backpack (top right). The backpack has four pins, VCC, GND, SDA and 
SCL, shown on its left edge in the photo. It takes power from the 5V rail and joins the shared I2C 
bus, so the ESP32 can write text to the display with two signal wires instead of the usual sixteen. 
The LCD shows the current heart rate, temperature, SpO2 and a status word, for example OK or 
an alert. 
LED and buzzer (right side of the breadboard). The red LED is driven from GPIO 18 through 
a 220 ohm current -limiting resistor to ground. The active buzzer is driven from GPIO 19 and 
returns to ground. When the ESP32 sets these pins HIGH the LED lights and the buzzer sounds. 
Neither depends on WiFi, so the alarm works offline. 
How the connected parts give results. In each sampling cycle of 2 to 5 seconds the ESP32 (1) 
reads the DS3231 for the current time, (2) reads the MAX30102 and calculates heart rate and 
SpO2, and (3) reads the MLX90614 for skin temperature. The three values are compa red against 
the neonatal threshold ranges held in the firmware. The result is written to the LCD in the same 
cycle, and if a value is out of range on consecutive samples the LED and buzzer are switched on. 
The timestamped reading is then stored in the loca l buffer and, when WiFi is available, sent as 
JSON over HTTP to the Flask REST API, from which the React dashboard displays it. In this way 
the local display and alarms give the result immediately at the bedside, while the network path 
adds the stored history and the AI risk scoring. 
3.3 Develop (Software) 
The development stage covers the firmware on the device, the Flask backend and database, the AI 
models and the React dashboard. The safety-critical alert path is developed to run entirely on the 
device, separate from everything that depends on the network. 
Specific objective: To develop the firmware, Flask and PostgreSQL backend with explainable AI 
risk scoring, and React dashboard that together provide on -device threshold alerts, offline 
buffering with automatic synchronisation, and risk-prioritised live and historical monitoring. The 
five parts below cover the firmware, buffering and synchronisation, database and API, AI scoring, 
and dashboard that make up this objective. 
3.3.1 Firmware: Timed Sampling and On-Device Threshold Alerts 
Part of the objective: To develop firmware that performs periodic, timed sampling and an on -
device threshold-checking algorithm that triggers local alerts within a bounded maximum latency. 
How it will be achieved: The firmware will sample heart rate, SpO2 and temperature at a fixed 
period of 2 to 5 seconds, scheduled with a hardware timer or a FreeRTOS task on the ESP32 rather 
14 
 
than with blocking delay() calls, so that the sampling period stays steady. Each reading will be 
compared against evidence -based neonatal ranges held as constants in the firmware, taken from 
the WHO temperature classes (mild, moderate and severe hypothermia) and the neonatal heart -
rate and SpO2 reference ranges cited in Chapter 2. When a value is out of range the firmware will 
switch on the buzzer and LED and show the alert on the LCD in the same sampling cycle, without 
any call to the network. To limit alarm fatigue, an alert will be raised only after a reading is out of 
range on consecutive samples and will clear when readings return to range. The maximum allowed 
delay between a threshold breach and the alert will be set as a design bound (no more than one 
sampling period plus processing time) and checked in testing. 
3.3.2 Local Buffering and Synchronisation 
Part of the objective: To develop local buffering of readings during connectivity loss, with 
automatic synchronisation to the Flask backend once WiFi is restored. 
How it will be achieved: Every reading will be stamped with the DS3231 time and the session ID 
and stored in a circular buffer in ESP32 memory (or on the optional microSD module, if used). 
The firmware will check WiFi state on every cycle and log each online/offline transition. While 
the device is offline it will continue sampling and alerting locally and keep adding readings to the 
buffer. When WiFi returns, the buffered readings will be sent to the backend in batches as JSON 
over HTTP in their original order, and a reading will be removed from the buffer only after the 
backend confirms it has been stored. If the buffer fills, the oldest readings will be overwritten and 
the loss will be counted and reported on the next sync. 
3.3.3 Database and REST API 
Part of the objective: To develop a PostgreSQL -backed data model and Flask REST API 
supporting multiple devices and patients. 
How it will be achieved: An entity-relationship diagram will model patients (identified by an 
anonymised patient code), devices, monitoring sessions, readings and alerts, with the session 
acting as the link between a device and a patient so that a device can be reused across patients. The 
tables will be created in PostgreSQL through Flask -SQLAlchemy models. The Flask REST API 
will provide endpoints to register devices and patients, start and end sessions, receive single or 
batched readings, and return readings, alerts and risk scores to the dashboard. Requests will be 
validated and identified by device credentials, and duplicate readings arriving after a re-sent batch 
will be rejected using the device ID and timestamp. 
3.3.4 Explainable Risk Scoring and Trend Detection 
Part of the objective: To develop an explainable backend composite risk -scoring model and a 
rolling-window trend-anomaly detector, distinct from the safety-critical local alert path. 
How it will be achieved: Both AI components run only on the backend, never on the device alert 
path. A composite risk score will be produced by logistic regression trained with scikit -learn, 
15 
 
chosen over black-box models so that each score can be explained from its coefficients. Becau se 
no real clinical dataset is available, training data will be generated synthetically from normal 
sessions and simulated deterioration scenarios, labelled using the clinical thresholds, and this is 
stated openly as a limitation. The trained model will be saved with joblib and loaded by the Flask 
service. The trend detector will run over a rolling window of the most recent readings and flag a 
consistent directional decline (for example, temperature falling or SpO2 drifting down) before any 
single threshold is crossed. Each score will be stored together with the main contributing factors 
so the dashboard can show why a patient was ranked high. 
3.3.5 React Dashboard 
Part of the objective: To develop a React -based dashboard presenting live and historical data,  
sorted and prioritised by AI-derived risk. 
How it will be achieved: The dashboard will be built in React and will poll the Flask API at a 
fixed interval. Polling is used instead of persistent WebSocket connections because it degrades 
more predictably on intermittent rural connections. It will show a patient list ordered by risk score, 
live and historical charts of heart rate, SpO2 and temperature drawn with Chart.js (with alert events 
marked using chartjs-plugin-annotation), the device connectivity state, and the explanation behind 
each risk score. Wireframes will be drawn first and refined with feedback from the group and 
supervisor. 
3.4 Test 
Testing follows the layered approach of the iterative development process: individual hardware 
tests first, then software tests, then the full pipeline. Test cases, expected results and actual results 
will be recorded in test tables so that the data can be used in the evaluation stage. 
Specific objective: To test the hardware components and the software (device sketch,  backend 
and dashboard), individually and as an integrated system, through connectivity, end -to-end 
dataflow, API and security tests. The hardware tests come first, followed by the software tests and 
then the integrated end-to-end tests. 
3.4.1 Hardware Testing 
Part of the objective: To test the hardware components, individually and when integrated, to 
confirm that each functions correctly. 
How it will be achieved: Each component will be tested on its own before integration: the ESP32 
(upload, serial output,  GPIO and WiFi), the MAX30102 and MLX90614 (I2C detection and 
readings compared against a reference clinical -grade pulse oximeter and digital thermometer on 
volunteers), the DS3231 (time kept across a power cycle, and drift checked over an extended 
period), the LCD (correct display of heart rate and temperature), and the buzzer and LED 
(activation on command). An integration test will then run all components together on the shared 
I2C bus to check for address conflicts and power problems. Simulated out-of-range conditions will 
16 
 
be used to confirm that the buzzer, LED and LCD alerts activate and clear as designed. Any failed 
component will be replaced or rewired and retested before software testing begins. 
3.4.2 Software Testing 
Part of the objective: To test the software (the device sketch, backend and dashboard) for 
connectivity, end-to-end dataflow, API correctness and security. 
How it will be achieved: The following tests will be carried out: 
● Connectivity tests: the sketch is tested for WiFi connection, reconnection after the access 
point is switched off and on, and correct logging of online/offline transitions. 
● Internet connectivity tests: the device and the dashboard are tested for reaching the backend 
over the network, including behaviour when WiFi is connected but there is no internet 
access, to confirm that buffering starts and local alerts continue. 
● Dataflow tests: readings are traced from the sensor through the device, the HTTP channel, 
the Flask API and PostgreSQL to the dashboard, and back (for example, configuration and 
acknowledgements from the backend to the device), checking that values, timestamps and 
ordering are preserved. Buffered readings from an offline period are checked for 
completeness and order after synchronisation. 
● API tests: every endpoint is tested with valid, missing, malformed and duplicate input using 
Postman or automated Python tests, checking status codes, response formats and database 
contents. 
● Security tests: checks that endpoints reject requests without valid device credent ials, that 
inputs are sanitised against injection, that patient records contain no real names, and that 
credentials and secrets are not exposed in the code repository or in responses. 
● Dashboard tests: correct display of live and historical data, risk -based ordering, alert 
markers and behaviour when the backend is unreachable. 
3.4.3 Test Data and Sampling Metrics 
Objective: To define the data that will be sampled during testing, and how it will be measured, so 
that the system can be evaluated objectively from the results it produces. 
How it will be achieved: Every test will record its inputs, the expected result and the actual result 
in a test table, with each row time -stamped from the DS3231 clock. Device readings, reference 
readings and timing measurements will be saved as CSV files so they can be analysed in Python 
(pandas) without manual copying. Human -subject measurements will use 5 adult student 
volunteers who give verbal consent, and any simulated data will be labelled as simulated. The table 
below lists, for each metric, what is sampled, how much, what it is compared with, and what result

3.5 Evaluate 
3.5.1 Evaluation of Test Data and Metrics 
Part of the objective: To evaluate the data obtained from the tests and present the metrics 
involved. 
How it will be achieved: The data recorded during testing, as specified in Section 3.4.3, will be 
analysed and reported with the following metrics: 
● Sampling accuracy: The difference between device readings and the reference instruments, 
reported as mean absolute error. 
● Sampling period and jitter: The interval between consecutive samples, logged with GPIO 
or serial timestamps over a long run, reported as mean, maximum and standard deviation 
against the configured period. 
● On-device alert latency: The time from a simulated threshold breach to the buzzer and LED 
activating, measured over repeated trials and compared with the design bound. 
● Synchronisation and dataflow performance: The share of buffered readings correctly 
delivered, and the time to clear a buffer after WiFi is restored. 
20 
 
● AI layer against a threshold -only baseline: Both approaches are run over the same 
simulated scenarios (normal, gradual deterioration and sudden deterioration), comparing 
false-alarm rate and detection lead time. 
● Alert correctness and timekeeping: The share of test vectors that produced the expected 
alert decision, and the DS3231 drift in seconds per day. 
● Backend and dashboard performance: API pass rate, mean and 95th -percentile response 
time, security checks passed, and end-to-end delay from device to dashboard. 
● Continuous operation: unexpected resets and uptime over the long run. 
Results will be presented in tables and charts, compared with the design requirements and with the 
benchmarks from the reviewed literature (for example, the sensitivity reported for the BEMPU 
TempWatch). Limitations will be stated, in particular the use of synthetic data rather than real 
neonates.

3.8 Ethical Considerations 
● No real neonates are used in testing at any stage. Testing uses student volunteers or clearly 
labelled simulated data. 
● Patient records use an anonymised patient_code identifier rather than real names, by 
design. 
● The device and dashboard are explicitly presented as a screening/early -warning aid, not a 
diagnostic tool, in all documentation and any demonstration to clinical staff. 
● Any future extension toward real clinical deployment or data collection would require 
formal ethical review and informed consent, which is outside the scope of this course 
project.
