ABSTRACT
This report presented the design, implementation, testing and evaluation of an offline-first neonatal vital-sign monitoring prototype. The implemented system used an ESP32-S3 with a MAX30102 pulse-oximetry sensor, MLX90614 non-contact temperature sensor, DS1302 timekeeping interface, ST7789 SPI display, microSD storage, RGB indicator and buzzer. The firmware acquired vital-sign data, applied local threshold logic, displayed system status, stored readings locally and synchronised queued records through an authenticated backend when Wi-Fi was available. The backend used REST services, PostgreSQL models, authentication and a React dashboard for live and historical monitoring.
Kaggle-derived datasets were audited and de-identified, but their source labels were not verified as clinical deterioration outcomes. The deployed AI model was therefore trained on 480 controlled synthetic monitoring sessions covering eight scenarios. The data was divided into 288 training, 96 validation and 96 test sessions. On 19,272 held-out synthetic rows, the model achieved precision of 0.9377, recall of 0.8177 and ROC-AUC of 0.9748. It detected 44 of 49 simulated events at onset with a median lead time of 107.5 seconds. Compared with the threshold-only baseline, the AI detected more simulated events earlier but produced more warning episodes per hour.
Firmware compilation and upload passed, repeated SD diagnostics passed, runtime, heartbeat and synchronisation requests returned HTTP 200, and the validated SPI carrier recorded zero reported ERC, DRC, unrouted or schematic-parity violations. Heart-rate stability, reference-instrument accuracy and clinical validation were not established. The project therefore produced a reproducible research prototype rather than a clinically deployable diagnostic device.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background
Neonatal health required close observation because newborns could experience rapid changes in temperature, oxygen saturation and heart rate. Two conditions of particular concern were neonatal hypothermia and hypoxia.
Neonatal hypothermia occurred when the newborn’s body temperature fell below the recommended range. The proposal used the following WHO temperature categories:
Mild hypothermia: 36.0–36.49 °C.
Moderate hypothermia: 32.0–35.99 °C.
Severe hypothermia: below 32.0 °C.
Hypoxia or low oxygen saturation represented another potentially serious condition. A decline in SpO₂ could indicate respiratory compromise and could occur together with changes in heart rate.
Evidence from Northern Uganda demonstrated the importance of this problem. Mukunya et al. reported a 51.0% incidence of neonatal hypothermia among 1,330 neonates in Lira District [1]. The study identified low birth weight, home birth and delayed breastfeeding initiation as risk factors.
The study relied on periodic measurements rather than continuous monitoring. This meant that a change occurring between observation visits could remain undetected. This limitation was important in rural facilities where staffing, electricity and network connectivity could be constrained.
The project therefore implemented an offline-first monitoring prototype. It used an ESP32-S3 to collect vital-sign data from embedded sensors, apply local threshold rules, display the current status, activate local alerts and store readings locally. When network connectivity was available, the device synchronised readings with a backend for storage, history, dashboard visualisation and experimental AI risk scoring.
The final implemented revision used:
MAX30102 for heart-rate and SpO₂ signal acquisition.
MLX90614 for non-contact temperature acquisition.
ST7789 for local graphical display.
SD storage for offline logging.
RGB output and buzzer for local status and warning indication.
ESP32-S3 for processing and communication.
Backend API and PostgreSQL for persistence.
React dashboard for monitoring and review.
Explainable logistic-regression risk scoring for synthetic research evaluation.
The final implementation differed from the original proposal in some hardware details. The proposal described a 16x2 I2C LCD and DS3231 arrangement, while the implemented firmware used an ST7789 SPI display and a DS1302 pin arrangement. These differences were recorded in the report.
1.2 Problem Statement
Rural and resource-limited health facilities could lack affordable continuous monitoring systems capable of observing several neonatal vital signs at the same time.
Existing systems could also depend on:
Frequent manual observation.
Continuous electricity.
Continuous network connectivity.
Expensive commercial monitoring equipment.
Cloud services for storage and analysis.
When connectivity was unavailable, a cloud-dependent system could fail to transmit readings or provide remote analysis. When staff observations were intermittent, abnormal changes could be detected late.
The project therefore addressed the need for a low-cost embedded monitor that could:
Measure more than one neonatal vital sign.
Generate local alerts without internet access.
Store readings during offline periods.
Synchronise records when connectivity returned.
Provide backend history and dashboard visualisation.
Provide experimental AI-assisted risk scoring and trend interpretation.
Preserve a clear separation between prototype research output and clinical diagnosis.

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Introduction
This chapter reviewed the evidence supporting the development of an offline-first neonatal vital-sign monitoring system with AI-assisted early warning. The review focused on five themes:
The prevalence and consequences of neonatal hypothermia and hypoxia.
The limitations of intermittent manual monitoring.
Existing neonatal monitoring devices and connected systems.
The use of artificial intelligence in neonatal monitoring and hypothermia management.
The research gap addressed by the NeoGuard project.
The reviewed literature guided the hardware selection, local alert strategy, offline-storage design, AI design and evaluation metrics [1–5].
2.2 Neonatal Hypothermia and Hypoxia
Neonatal hypothermia is a significant concern because newborns have limited ability to regulate body temperature. The proposal used the WHO temperature categories:
Mild hypothermia: 36.0–36.49 °C.
Moderate hypothermia: 32.0–35.99 °C.
Severe hypothermia: below 32.0 °C.
Mukunya et al. reported a 51.0% incidence of neonatal hypothermia among 1,330 neonates in Lira District, Northern Uganda [1]. The study identified home birth, low birth weight and delayed breastfeeding initiation as important risk factors.
The monitoring approach used in that study relied on periodic home visits and digital thermometer spot checks. The measurements were therefore intermittent rather than continuous. A newborn could experience a clinically important temperature change between visits without the change being detected immediately.
Hypoxia or low oxygen saturation represents another important neonatal danger. Oxygen saturation and heart rate can change rapidly during respiratory compromise. A system that monitors temperature alone may therefore miss deterioration that first appears through oxygenation or pulse changes.
The reviewed evidence consequently supported a multi-parameter system that could monitor:
Temperature.
Oxygen saturation.
Heart rate.
The project used these parameters because they could be measured using compact embedded sensors and could be processed locally without laboratory equipment.
The system was designed as an early-warning aid. It did not diagnose hypothermia, hypoxia, sepsis or any other medical condition.
2.3 Existing Monitoring Solutions
2.3.1 BEMPU TempWatch
McAbee et al. evaluated the BEMPU TempWatch wearable temperature-alarm bracelet in Ghana [2]. The study included 1,973 temperature readings from 249 neonates.
The device achieved:
Sensitivity: 67.8%.
Specificity: 95.9%.
Sensitivity was lower among:
Neonates in incubators: 58.4%.
Very-low-birth-weight neonates: 62.8%.
The BEMPU TempWatch was important because it demonstrated that an offline temperature-alarm device could be used in a low-resource African setting. It also provided a real-world performance benchmark.
However, the device was primarily temperature-based. Its results showed that a single-parameter threshold device could miss some hypothermia episodes. This motivated the NeoGuard design to combine temperature with heart rate and SpO₂ and to include trend analysis rather than relying only on one temperature threshold.
2.3.2 Sensor Mobile Cloud Computing Neonatal Monitor
De et al. proposed a neonatal health-monitoring system based on Sensor Mobile Cloud Computing [3]. The system combined temperature, acceleration and heart-rate sensors with wireless communication, a cloud backend and Android-based alerts.
The system demonstrated the value of multi-parameter monitoring and remote reporting. However, the design depended on wireless transmission and cloud services for continued processing and storage.
This dependency presented a limitation for the rural context targeted by NeoGuard. If the network connection failed, the system could not provide the same level of cloud-based monitoring. NeoGuard therefore retained local threshold alerts and local buffering on the embedded device.
2.3.3 IoT Neonatal Incubator Monitoring
Abd El-Aziz and Taloba developed an IoT-based neonatal-incubator monitoring system using an Arduino Uno [4]. The system monitored pulse rate, temperature, humidity and gas-related conditions and transmitted data wirelessly to clinical staff.
The work demonstrated that embedded devices could monitor several environmental and physiological variables. However, the system was designed around an incubator and was not primarily intended as a portable ward-level monitor.
The system also assumed continued wireless connectivity. NeoGuard differed by treating connectivity as optional: local alerting and storage continued first, while backend synchronisation occurred when the network became available.
2.3.4 Artificial Intelligence in Neonatal Hypothermia Management
Rahmawati et al. reviewed nine studies applying artificial intelligence to neonatal hypothermia management [5]. The review found that many AI applications focused on fuzzy-logic control of incubator temperature and humidity.
Other AI applications used laboratory values for tasks such as sepsis diagnosis. These laboratory inputs were not necessarily available at a low-resource point of care.
The reviewed work did not identify a system that combined:
Multi-parameter bedside monitoring.
Offline-first operation.
Local threshold alarms.
Explainable trend-based AI.
Portable use in rural Ugandan facilities.
This observation guided the NeoGuard AI design. The model was implemented as a backend risk-scoring and trend-analysis component, while the safety-critical alert path remained on the ESP32.

2.5 Research Gap
The review identified four main gaps.
2.5.1 Lack of combined offline-first and multi-parameter monitoring
The BEMPU TempWatch demonstrated offline operation but focused on temperature. Other reviewed systems monitored several variables but depended on continuous connectivity.
NeoGuard combined:
Temperature.
Heart rate.
SpO₂.
Local threshold alerts.
SD-card buffering.
Later synchronisation.
2.5.2 Dependence on cloud connectivity
The SMCC and incubator-monitoring systems relied on wireless or cloud services. This created a weakness in environments where network access was intermittent.
NeoGuard addressed this by keeping the safety-critical alert path on the ESP32. The display, RGB indicator and buzzer could continue to operate without backend access.
2.5.3 Limited explainable AI for bedside early warning
The reviewed AI literature focused mainly on incubator climate control or laboratory-based prediction. It did not provide a direct example of an explainable, trend-based risk score using only compact bedside vital-sign inputs.
NeoGuard implemented a logistic-regression risk model that returned:
A risk score.
A warning state.
Feature contributions.
Trend flags.
Model version and validation scope.
2.5.4 Limited rural Ugandan design focus
The reviewed literature did not describe a system designed specifically around:
Intermittent connectivity.
Low-cost embedded hardware.
Local warning.
Offline storage.
Portable multi-parameter monitoring.
Explainable backend analysis.
NeoGuard was designed to investigate this combination in the context of rural Ugandan healthcare.
2.6 Summary
The literature established that neonatal hypothermia and hypoxia required timely monitoring. The Northern Uganda evidence showed a high burden of neonatal hypothermia and demonstrated the limitations of intermittent manual measurements [1].
The BEMPU TempWatch demonstrated the value of standalone temperature alarms but also showed that a single-parameter system could miss episodes [2]. The SMCC and IoT incubator systems demonstrated multi-parameter wireless monitoring but depended more strongly on connectivity [3], [4]. The AI literature showed increasing interest in automated neonatal management but focused mainly on incubator control or laboratory-based prediction [5].
The literature therefore supported the NeoGuard architecture:
Multi-parameter sensing.
Local threshold alerting.
Offline buffering.
Optional backend synchronisation.
Explainable AI risk scoring.
Trend-based early warning.
The review also established the limits of the contribution. NeoGuard was a prototype designed to address an engineering gap. Its AI results were based on synthetic data, and its sensor measurements were not clinically validated. Therefore, the system was presented as a screening and early-warning aid rather than a replacement for clinical judgement.

CHAPTER 3: SYSTEM DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY
3.1 Overview of the System
The system followed a design, develop, test and evaluate process.
During the design stage, the required sensing, local-alert, storage, communication, database, AI and dashboard functions were identified. The hardware interfaces were assigned to the ESP32-S3 and represented in the KiCad schematic and PCB files.
During development, the firmware was implemented first so that the sensors, display, indicators, timekeeping and SD storage could be tested independently. The backend was then implemented to receive readings, validate data, store records and expose monitoring information. The AI service was implemented as a backend prototype, while the React dashboard was implemented as the staff-facing interface.
During testing, the system was tested at component level and integrated-system level. Firmware build logs, serial-monitor evidence, SD diagnostics, backend tests, API smoke tests, AI reports and KiCad verification records were used.
During evaluation, the project compared the recorded evidence with the proposal objectives. The evaluation distinguished between functional prototype success, measured performance, incomplete measurements and clinical limitations.

3.2 System Architecture
The implemented architecture contained five main layers:
Sensing layer: MAX30102 and MLX90614 sensors.
Embedded-control layer: ESP32-S3 firmware, display, RGB indicator, buzzer, timekeeping and SD storage.
Communication layer: Wi-Fi and HTTP/JSON synchronisation.
Backend layer: authenticated API, PostgreSQL database, alerts and risk scoring.
User-interface layer: React dashboard displaying live values, history, alerts and AI explanations.
Figure 3.1: System hardware schematic and signal connections
The schematic showed the ESP32-S3 as the central controller. The MAX30102 and MLX90614 were connected through the shared I2C bus on GPIO4 and GPIO5. The DS1302 used GPIO6, GPIO7 and GPIO14. The ST7789 used SPI-related GPIO8–GPIO12. The SD interface used GPIO21, GPIO40, GPIO41 and GPIO42. The buzzer and RGB outputs used GPIO15–GPIO18.
The schematic also showed the common ground connections and the separation between sensor acquisition, local alerting, display output and storage.
Figure 3.2: Full system architecture from sensors to backend, AI and dashboard
The end-to-end data flow was:
The MAX30102 measured optical pulse and oxygenation signals.
The MLX90614 measured infrared surface temperature.
The ESP32-S3 validated and processed the readings.
The firmware applied local threshold rules.
The display, RGB indicator and buzzer were updated.
The reading was timestamped and written to SD storage.
If Wi-Fi was available, the reading was sent as HTTP/JSON.
The backend authenticated and validated the request.
PostgreSQL stored the device, session, reading and alert information.
The AI service calculated a prototype risk score and trend flags.
The React dashboard displayed values, history, alerts and explanations.
The local alert path was independent of the backend and AI. Therefore, a network outage or unavailable model did not disable the ESP32 warning path.

3.4 Hardware Design
3.4.1 Component Selection
The MAX30102 was selected because it provided compact optical pulse and oxygenation sensing. The MLX90614 was selected because it provided non-contact infrared temperature sensing. The ESP32-S3 was selected because it provided processing, GPIO, Wi-Fi and support for the required embedded firmware.
SD storage was selected for offline retention. The ST7789 was used in the final implemented revision because it provided a larger graphical display than the original proposal LCD. The RGB indicator and buzzer were retained for local warning and status feedback.
The final hardware therefore differed from the initial proposal in the display, timekeeping implementation and pin assignments.

CHAPTER 6: AI MODEL DEVELOPMENT, TRAINING, AND EVALUATION
6.1 Introduction
The artificial-intelligence component was developed to provide backend-assisted early warning for possible neonatal deterioration. It was designed to analyse temperature, oxygen-saturation and heart-rate patterns and to identify abnormal conditions either at the current observation or within a short future window.
The AI system was implemented as a decision-support component. It did not replace the safety-critical alert logic running on the ESP32. The local ESP32 firmware continued to generate its own warning states through the display, RGB indicator and buzzer, even when Wi-Fi, the backend or the AI model was unavailable.
The AI model was not presented as a diagnostic system. Its purpose was to demonstrate how an embedded neonatal monitor could combine current vital-sign values with recent trends to produce an explainable risk score for further attention by a health worker.
6.2 Data Collection From Kaggle
Two source datasets were available in the project workspace as archive files obtained from Kaggle.
The first dataset was stored in archive.zip and contained the file newborn_health_monitoring_with_risk.csv. It contained 3,000 records representing 100 de-identified baby identifiers. The dataset included temperature, heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, birth and growth measurements, feeding information, jaundice level, Apgar score, and a risk-level field.
The second dataset was stored in archive_1.zip and contained the file Hypothermia.csv. It contained 200 records with temperature observations and related case and timing fields.
The original Kaggle URLs and licence information were not preserved in the project workspace.

6.3 Decision to Use Synthetic Training Sessions
The Kaggle datasets were not used as the direct training data for the deployed NeoGuard model. This decision was made because the available datasets did not provide a verified continuous neonatal monitoring sequence with reliable event timestamps.
The source risk labels were not documented as confirmed clinical deterioration outcomes. In addition, the hypothermia dataset contained unverified temperature field meanings and incomplete later measurements. Training a clinical-warning model directly from those labels could therefore have produced misleading results.
For this reason, the project separated the Kaggle archive work from the deployed prototype model:
The Kaggle files were audited and de-identified.
The archived records were staged as review-only research data.
An exploratory archive benchmark was created but was explicitly marked as “never deployed.”
The deployed prototype model was trained on controlled synthetic monitoring sessions with known scenario labels and known event times.
This separation prevented unverified source labels from being presented as clinical truth.
6.4 Exploratory Benchmark Using the Kaggle Data
A separate exploratory benchmark was generated from the staged newborn archive. It used temperature, heart rate and oxygen saturation as input features and attempted to reproduce the source At Risk label.
After excluding seven invalid oxygen-saturation records, the data was split by baby identifier into 60 training subjects, 20 validation subjects and 20 test subjects. The test portion contained 600 rows, including 78 positive rows.
The exploratory benchmark produced precision of 1.0000, recall of 0.0256, average precision of 0.2649, ROC-AUC of 0.6034 and Brier score of 0.1093. Its confusion matrix was TN=522, FP=0, FN=76 and TP=2.
These results showed that the source label could not be treated as a reliable clinical target. Although the benchmark produced no false positives at the selected threshold, it detected only two of the 78 positive test rows. The benchmark was therefore retained for research review only and was not deployed in the backend.
6.5 Synthetic Dataset Generation
The deployed model was trained using synthetic monitoring sessions generated by ai/train.py.
The generator created 480 independent sessions using the fixed random seed 20261009. Each session represented one simulated neonatal-monitoring scenario. Eight scenarios were used:
Normal
Cooling
Desaturation
Combined deterioration
Recovery
Motion-artifact-like observations
Near-threshold observations
Sensor dropout
There were 60 sessions for each scenario.
Each generated session covered 1,200 seconds and produced one observation every five seconds. The generator therefore simulated a time series rather than isolated independent records.
The initial physiological values were randomly generated within defined ranges:
Temperature was generated between 36.7 °C and 37.3 °C.
SpO₂ was generated between 95.5% and 99%.
Heart rate was generated between 115 bpm and 150 bpm.
A deterioration onset time was randomly selected between 200 and 500 seconds. Cooling scenarios gradually reduced temperature. Desaturation scenarios gradually reduced SpO₂ and increased heart rate. Combined scenarios applied both changes. Recovery scenarios introduced a deterioration phase followed by gradual recovery.
Noise was added to the observed values to represent sensor variation:
Temperature noise was added using a normal distribution.
SpO₂ noise was added and clipped to the range 50–100%.
Heart-rate noise was added before rounding.
Artifact scenarios changed the quality field to motion_artifact.
Dropout scenarios changed the quality field to sensor_error and removed the SpO₂ value for the affected interval.
The latent physiological values were kept separate from noisy observed values. This was important because the event label represented the simulated physiological condition rather than a sensor artefact.
6.6 Synthetic Event Labelling
A synthetic event was labelled when the latent temperature fell below 36.5 °C or the latent SpO₂ fell below 90%.
The model target was:
A simulated low-temperature or low-oxygen event occurring at the current observation or within the next 120 seconds.
The label horizon was therefore 120 seconds. The final 120 seconds of each session were excluded from training rows when a complete future label horizon was not available.
The labels were not clinical diagnoses. They were generated from the known rules used to create the synthetic scenarios.
6.7 Data Pre-processing
Before a row was allowed to enter the model-training dataset, the feature extractor applied the following checks:
Readings were sorted by timestamp.
Only the causal 120-second history before the scoring time was used.
Future readings were excluded.
Duplicate timestamps were rejected.
At least 12 valid readings were required for each vital sign.
At least 60 seconds of time coverage was required.
Gaps greater than 15 seconds were rejected.
The latest reading had to have quality marked as ok.
Temperature values had to remain between 30 °C and 42 °C.
SpO₂ values had to remain between 50% and 100%.
Heart-rate values had to remain between 40 bpm and 220 bpm.
Missing, invalid or poor-quality terminal readings were rejected.
These checks prevented the model from scoring incomplete, stale, future-leaking or obviously invalid windows.

The final model used 12 features. Four features were calculated for each of the three vital signs.

6.9 Model Selection and Training
A logistic-regression model was selected because it provided a simple and explainable relationship between the extracted features and the risk score.
The training process was:
Synthetic sessions were generated.
Valid causal feature windows were extracted.
Feature values were standardised using training-set means and scales.
Sessions were split by session identifier rather than by individual rows.
The split produced 288 training sessions, 96 validation sessions and 96 test sessions.
Logistic regression was fitted on the training sessions.
Validation sessions were used to select the operating threshold.
The test sessions were kept separate until final evaluation.
The trained coefficients, intercept, means, scales and threshold were written to ai/artifacts/risk_model.json.
The backend loaded the JSON model and verified its feature contract before scoring.
The decision threshold was 0.75. The model returned a probability-like score between 0 and 1. A score at or above 0.75 produced a high-risk warning; a lower score produced a low-risk result.

7.8 Discussion of Results
The project achieved stronger evidence for system integration than for clinical measurement performance. The firmware, storage, backend and AI data paths were implemented and tested. The most important architectural achievement was the separation between local safety alerts and network-dependent services.
The AI evaluation showed that the model detected more simulated events earlier than the threshold-only baseline. However, the AI also produced more warning episodes per simulated hour. This demonstrated a sensitivity-versus-warning-burden trade-off rather than universal superiority.
The hardware testing also revealed important limitations. The MAX30102 generated usable raw signals, but the heart-rate estimates were unstable. The temperature sensor produced changing readings, but the warm-target interaction rule did not trigger under the tested conditions. These results prevented the system from being described as clinically accurate.

CHAPTER 8: CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS
8.1 Conclusion
The project designed and implemented an offline-first neonatal vital-sign monitoring prototype that combined embedded sensing, local alerting, offline storage, Wi-Fi synchronisation, backend persistence, dashboard visualisation and exploratory artificial-intelligence risk scoring.
The implemented hardware used an ESP32-S3 as the central controller. The MAX30102 provided optical pulse and oxygenation signals, while the MLX90614 provided non-contact temperature readings. The final implementation also included an ST7789 SPI display, SD storage, RGB status indication, buzzer output and a DS1302-style timekeeping interface.
The final firmware performed the following operations:
Initialised the sensing and display hardware.
Read pulse, SpO₂ and temperature information.
Applied quality checks to incoming values.
Applied local threshold logic.
Updated the display and RGB indicators.
Controlled the buzzer path.
Stored readings locally.
Queued readings for later synchronisation.
Sent readings to the backend when Wi-Fi was available.
The local alert path remained independent of Wi-Fi, the backend and the AI model. This was an important design decision because a network outage or unavailable AI service did not automatically disable the device-level warning path.
The backend implemented authenticated REST routes, PostgreSQL persistence, device and session handling, alert storage, synchronisation logic and risk-score access. The React dashboard provided the intended path for live and historical monitoring.
The AI component was implemented as an exploratory backend service. Kaggle-derived datasets were audited and de-identified, but their source labels were not treated as verified clinical outcomes. The deployed model was therefore trained using 480 synthetic sessions across eight scenarios. It used causal time-series features and logistic regression to produce a risk score and explanation.
The synthetic test results showed:
Precision: 0.9377.
Recall/sensitivity: 0.8177.
ROC-AUC: 0.9748.
Average precision: 0.9475.
Brier score: 0.0476.
True positives: 4,395.
True negatives: 13,605.
False positives: 292.
False negatives: 980.
Simulated event sensitivity: 0.8980.
Median simulated lead time: 107.5 seconds.
False-warning episodes per simulated monitoring hour: 1.4197.
The AI detected more simulated events earlier than the threshold-only baseline. However, it also produced more warning episodes per hour and lower warning-episode precision. These results described synthetic scenarios only and did not establish clinical performance.
The hardware and software evidence showed that the prototype was integrated successfully at proof-of-concept level. Firmware compilation and upload passed, I2C sensor acquisition passed, display operation passed, repeated SD diagnostics passed, backend tests passed at the recorded checkpoint, and runtime, heartbeat and synchronisation requests returned HTTP 200 in the analysed runs. The validated SPI carrier PCB recorded zero reported ERC violations, DRC violations, unrouted items and schematic-parity differences.
The project did not establish:
Clinical temperature accuracy.
Clinical heart-rate accuracy.
Clinical SpO₂ accuracy.
Long-term battery performance.
Complete sampling jitter.
Measured alert latency.
Controlled offline-duration synchronisation results.
Full Wi-Fi reconnection performance.
Dashboard delay and database-consistency percentages.
Clinical AI calibration.
Medical-device certification.
The final conclusion was therefore that the project produced a reproducible embedded research prototype, not a clinically deployable diagnostic system.
